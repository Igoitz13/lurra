Leasing your land for solar panels: what to know

More and more owners receive a letter or a call from a developer that wants to lease their land for a solar farm. Before you reply, it helps to know what they are looking for, how much is usually paid and what to check in the contract.

What a developer looks for

  • Room on the grid. This is what matters most. The plant has to connect to a substation or a line with spare capacity, and that capacity is limited: it is allocated in order of application among the projects that request it. With no capacity nearby there is no project, however good the land is.
  • Distance to the substation. The closer it is, the less the connection line costs. Developers usually look for land within a few kilometres of a substation.
  • Area. A ground-mounted plant takes up about 2 hectares per megawatt, depending on the technology. Developers usually look for 10 hectares or more, often by combining several neighbouring plots.
  • Flat or south-facing land. Gentle slopes make the works cheaper; north-facing slopes produce less.
  • No environmental constraints. Natura 2000 sites, protected natural areas, flood zones and land crossed by drove roads (vías pecuarias) or belonging to public forests (montes públicos) make authorisation harder or rule it out.

How much is usually paid

The rent is agreed per hectare per year and depends on the area and the project. As a reference, the Spanish farming press has reported rents of €500 to €700 per hectare per year for projects in Aragon (2019) and €1,000 to €1,200 in Castile and León (an article from 2019 updated in 2024). While the project is going through the permitting process, before construction, a much smaller amount is usually paid and the owner can keep farming. These figures come from specific cases, not an official price: get several offers if you can.

What to check in the contract

  • Term and extensions. Contracts usually run for 25 to 30 years, with extensions at the company's request. Check who decides on an extension and on what terms.
  • What you are signing. It may be a lease, a surface right (derecho de superficie) or an option to sign one later. Each has different consequences in the Land Registry and for tax.
  • Payments before construction. Many contracts are signed one to three years before building starts. Agree what you are paid in the meantime and what happens if the project does not go ahead.
  • Rent review every year, in line with the consumer price index or by a fixed percentage.
  • Decommissioning. At the end, the land must be handed back clear of panels, foundations and cabling, and fit for farming, with a guarantee to secure this.
  • Easements and access rights that you grant, including over other plots you own.

Have a lawyer or the local farmers' organisation review the contract before you sign, and ask a tax adviser how the rent is taxed.

What needs to be authorised

A plant on rural land needs planning authorisation from the town hall or the autonomous community, depending on each region's law, an environmental assessment where required, and the electricity-sector authorisations. The Ministerio para la Transición Ecológica (Spain's environment ministry) publishes an environmental zoning map for renewables that shows how sensitive each area is: it is a first indication, not an authorisation.

How to tell whether your land has potential

The Lurra report includes the section “Solar panels: is it suitable for a solar farm?”, which brings together the solar radiation over the last ten years and the estimated output, the slope and aspect, the area and approximate megawatts, the nearest substation and high-voltage line, and whether the land is in a protected area or a flood zone. The decisive factor, spare grid capacity, is not open data: ask the distribution company for the area, which publishes capacity maps for its nodes.

If the plot is small, it may suit self-consumption for a farm or a house, or an energy community with the neighbours.
