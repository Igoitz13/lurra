The nota simple for a rural plot: how to request it and how to read it

The nota simple is an extract from the Registro de la Propiedad (the Spanish Land Registry). It is cheap, can be requested in minutes and is the document that tells you who the legal owner of the plot is and what charges it carries. You should see it before paying a deposit.

How to request it

  • Online, on the website of the Colegio de Registradores (the professional body of Spanish land registrars). It costs a few euros and usually arrives the same day.
  • In person, at the Land Registry office responsible for the municipality where the plot lies.

To request it you need the registry details (the registry and the property number, or the unique registry code, CRU, formerly called IDUFIR) or, if it is linked to the property, the cadastral reference. The seller has these details in their deed. You must state a legitimate interest, which in a purchase is obvious.

What it says

  • Description: where the plot is, the registered area, the boundaries (sometimes with the names of neighbours from decades ago) and, if there are any, the declared buildings.
  • Ownership: who the owner is, in what share and how they acquired it (purchase, inheritance, gift).
  • Charges: mortgages, seizures, easements (rights of way, aqueducts…), usufructs, registered leases, third-party rights and tax liens.
  • Coordination with the Catastro: whether the property has its graphic representation registered and whether it is coordinated with the Catastro (the Spanish cadastre). When it is, the registered shape and area match the cadastral ones.

What it does not say

  • What can be built: that is for the town hall to say. See the guide Can you build on a rural plot?
  • Whether the buildings have a licence, even if they are described.
  • Charges that have not been registered: a verbal lease, a track the neighbours use, unpaid bills.
  • The actual condition of the land.

The nota simple is for information only. The document with evidential value is the registry certificate (certificación registral), which costs more and is rarely needed: the notary checks the Registry just before the deed is signed.

Registry versus Catastro

With rural plots it is very common for the area in the Registry not to match the one in the Catastro, or for the boundaries to be described differently. The Registry says who the owner is; the Catastro says what the parcel looks like for the tax authorities. Neither is a topographic survey.

If the figures do not add up, compare them with the area measured on the ground or on the map. The Lurra report gives the cadastral area and the area measured on the outline: if the Registry figure is far from both, ask why before you sign. Differences can be corrected, but it takes time and money.

What to scrutinise

  • That the seller is the registered owner, or can prove the inheritance or purchase that has not yet been registered.
  • That there are no seizures or mortgages, or that they will be cancelled at the signing.
  • Easements: a right of way may be in favour of your plot (good) or against it (the neighbour crosses your land).
  • That the property is not unregistered, that is, never entered in the Registry at all. It happens in the countryside, and it complicates the purchase.
