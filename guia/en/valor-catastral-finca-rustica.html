Cadastral value of a rural plot: what it is, how to find it and what it is for

The cadastral value is the value that the Catastro (the Spanish cadastre) assigns to each property for tax purposes. For a rural plot it is usually far below what is paid for it, and it is not the figure that determines the taxes on the sale.

How it is calculated

The cadastral value adds together the value of the land and that of the buildings. On a rural plot, each part follows its own path:

  • The land keeps an old system, based on what each crop yielded according to its productive intensity. The values were set in 1990 using agricultural studies from the 1980s and since then have only been updated with the coefficients in the annual budget laws (leyes de presupuestos). That is why they bear little relation to the market.
  • The buildings (a house, a farm building, a storehouse) are valued under the same rules as urban ones when the municipality undergoes a general revaluation. The land they occupy is then valued using its own standard rates.

That is why the crops recorded in the Catastro matter: each sub-parcel, with its crop and intensity, contributes its share to the value. The guide to the Catastro for a rural plot explains how to read them.

Who can see it

In the territory covered by the national Catastro, the cadastral value is protected data, just like the owner's name and tax identification number (NIF). Anyone can look up a parcel's reference, area, crops and map, but not its cadastral value.

  • The owner can see it on the Sede Electrónica del Catastro (the Catastro's official website) with a digital certificate or an electronic identity card (DNI electrónico), or in the Catastro mobile app with Cl@ve (the Spanish government's electronic identification system). The IBI (property tax) bill usually shows it too.
  • A third party, such as a prospective buyer, needs the owner's express written consent. The law makes exceptions for notaries and registrars, heirs, and holders of rights in rem, leases or sharecropping agreements on the property. Adjoining neighbours can request data on the parcel, but not its cadastral value.

In practice, if you are buying, ask the seller for it: a cadastral certificate in their name or the latest IBI bill.

What it is for

  • IBI. It is the tax base for the IBI (Impuesto sobre Bienes Inmuebles, the annual property tax). Each town hall sets the rate, which for rural property ranges from 0.3% to 0.90%.
  • IRPF. Under IRPF (Spanish personal income tax), a rural plot is only taxed on deemed income if it has buildings that are not essential to the farming, livestock or forestry business. Land without buildings generates no deemed income.
  • Impuesto sobre el Patrimonio. For this wealth tax, the highest of the cadastral value, the value assessed by the tax authorities and the purchase price is used.
  • Transmisiones Patrimoniales, Sucesiones y Donaciones. For these taxes (transfer tax, and inheritance and gift tax), what counts is not the cadastral value but the reference value, unless the price or the declared value is higher.

The reference value

The reference value is set each year by the Dirección General del Catastro (the national directorate of the Catastro) on the basis of the prices in sales executed before a notary. For rural plots it starts from an average value per hectare for each crop group, with adjustments for location and for agronomic and socio-economic factors. It cannot exceed the market value.

  • It exists for rural plots without buildings. Plots with buildings have no reference value.
  • It is not protected data. It can be consulted on the Sede Electrónica del Catastro by identifying yourself with a digital certificate or Cl@ve, or with your DNI (Spanish identity card) at the Catastro's regional offices (gerencias) and through its Línea Directa helpline.
  • If the price you agree is lower than the reference value, the tax on the purchase (ITP, the property transfer tax) is calculated on the reference value. If you disagree with it, you can ask for your self-assessment to be corrected or appeal against the tax assessment.

The three values at a glance

  • Cadastral value: for tax purposes, protected. For rural land it comes from an old income-based system and is usually low. It is used for the IBI and the wealth tax.
  • Reference value: for tax purposes, open to consultation. It is derived from real prices in notarised sales. It serves as the minimum base for the ITP and for inheritance and gift tax.
  • Market price: the price agreed by buyer and seller. No body sets it; it depends on the plot, the water, the access and what you are allowed to do there.

Neither of the two tax values tells you whether the price is a good one. As a guide, the Lurra report gives the average price of each type of land in the autonomous community, from the Ministry of Agriculture's land price survey, but what a particular plot is worth depends on the plot itself: that is what the guide What to check before buying a rural plot covers.

Before you buy

  1. Ask for the cadastral reference and look up the plot's public data: area, crops and map. The Lurra report brings them together with what the Catastro does not tell you.
  2. If the plot has no buildings, look up its reference value and compare it with the price: it tells you the base on which you will pay the ITP.
  3. Ask the seller for the cadastral value, with a cadastral certificate or the IBI bill, so you know how much IBI you will pay.
  4. Check that what the Catastro says (crops, buildings, area) matches the nota simple (the Land Registry extract) and what you see on the ground.

Navarre and the Basque Country

The national Catastro does not operate in Navarre or the Basque Country: each territory has its own cadastre, with its own valuation and access rules, and its own taxes.

  • Navarre updates the cadastral value every year and, as a general rule, calculates it by comparison with the market.
  • In Bizkaia and Gipuzkoa, the foral cadastre regulations (normas forales) do not include the cadastral value among the protected data. Bizkaia also publishes a minimum attributable value that anyone can consult.
  • In Álava, ask the Diputación Foral (the provincial government).

For any question about your specific case, consult the Catastro's regional office, your town hall or a tax adviser.
