What to grow on a rural plot, depending on the climate

Before planting trees or buying land for a particular crop, it is worth looking at the climate. Four things decide almost everything: winter cold, spring frosts, summer heat and water.

Chill hours

Many fruit trees need a certain number of hours below about 7 °C during the winter to bud and flower properly. If they do not get them, flowering is uneven and the harvest falls. These are rough figures that vary a lot with the variety:

  • Almond: about 200 to 600 hours.
  • Peach and plum: about 400 to 1,000 hours; cherry, more.
  • Apple and pear: about 800 hours or more.
  • Walnut and pistachio: about 500 to 1,000 hours or more.

In mild climates, choose “low-chill” varieties.

Frost

Hard winter frosts limit sensitive crops: citrus and avocado suffer at a few degrees below zero, and olive trees suffer in hard frosts. Spring frosts, when the tree is in flower, are the ones that ruin the most harvests: almond trees flower early, and in an area with late frosts they will lose their blossom in many years. Within the same district, a valley floor gets considerably more frost than a hillside.

Summer heat

Growing degree days, the sum of degrees above 10 °C over the growing season, tell you whether a crop will ripen. Vines need a minimum amount of heat, and pistachios need long, hot summers. Cool, damp summers, on the other hand, encourage fungal diseases in olives, vines and almonds.

Rain and water

What matters is not only the year's rainfall but how much water the soil loses through evaporation and through plants. With little rain, many crops are only viable with irrigation, and that depends on having water: a registered well, a water concession or rights in an irrigation community (comunidad de regantes). Meadows need plentiful rain spread through the year, including the summer.

Soil matters too

Some crops have clear soil requirements. Chestnut, for example, does not tolerate limestone soils. Depth, stoniness and drainage weigh as much as the climate.

How to check your plot

The Lurra report includes the climate of the last ten years at the plot's location: rainfall, temperatures, frost days, typical dates of the last and first frost, chill hours, growing degree days and evapotranspiration, with a table for each month. With that data and the soil pH, it rates twelve crops (cereals, olive, vineyard, almond, pistachio, stone fruit, pome fruit, walnut, chestnut, pasture, citrus and avocado) as “suitable”, “with limitations” or “not suitable”, and says why.

It is a first indication: the data come from a model with cells several kilometres across, and in valleys and on slopes the real climate may differ. Before planting, ask an agronomist or the local agricultural office, and ask the neighbours what works there.
