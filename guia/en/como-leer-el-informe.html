How to read the Lurra report

The report has a summary with a traffic light and nine sections. Here is what each one means and how far it goes.

The traffic light

  • Green: the sources consulted show nothing that calls for special attention.
  • Amber: there is something worth clarifying before you go further, such as an environmental protection, a steep slope or an infrequent flood zone.
  • Red: there is a serious constraint, such as a frequent (10-year) flood zone on the plot.
  • Incomplete: a source did not respond and an important piece of data is missing. Try again later.

The traffic light tells you where to start reading. It is not a verdict: a plot in red may be a good buy for grazing, and one in green may have a problem that does not show up on these maps.

1. Cadastral record

The data from the Catastro, the Spanish cadastre (area, use, crops by sub-parcel), and the area measured on the outline. If they differ a lot, ask why. It also includes an indicative planning classification (urban, developable or non-developable land) taken from the Sistema de Información Urbana (Urban Information System) of the Ministerio de Vivienda (Spain's housing ministry), which summarises the planning data that municipalities report: it tells you at the outset what type of land the plot is on, but it does not distinguish ordinary rural land from protected rural land, nor does it always reflect the latest changes. The guide to building on rural land explains what to ask.

2. Terrain and access

Elevation, slope and aspect, from the 5 m terrain model of the IGN (Instituto Geográfico Nacional, Spain's national mapping agency). Slope is given as a percentage: 20% means 20 m of rise for every 100 m. Outside Spain a less detailed global model is used, and the report says so.

Below that, the nearest features according to OpenStreetMap, in any country: the track and the paved road, the village, the doctor, the pharmacy, the school and the power line, measured in a straight line from the edge of the plot. If there is no mapped track next to the plot, the traffic light flags it: ask how you get in and whether there is a right of way.

3. Water: flooding and groundwater

The area of the plot inside the official 10-, 100- and 500-year flood zones. “No” means that there is no mapped flood extent over the parcel. The guide to flood-prone plots explains the limits. Outside Spain there is no common official mapping: the report gives a first indication using a global 100-year river flood model (GloFAS, from Copernicus), with cells of about 150 m and covering only large rivers.

In the European Union, Norway and Iceland, the report adds the status of the groundwater bodies beneath the plot, according to the river basin management plans. If the aquifer is in poor quantitative status, more water is being abstracted than is recharged, and a new well may not be authorised.

4. Environmental protection

Natura 2000 sites and protected natural areas that touch the plot. More in the guide to Natura 2000. Outside Spain, the report queries Protected Planet, the world database of protected areas, with the designations that each country reports.

5. Wildfires and public land

Wildfires that have affected the plot, according to the burned areas mapped by satellite: in Europe and neighbouring areas, from the European EFFIS system since 2000; in the rest of the world, from the global GWIS system since 2012. The report gives the date, the area of the fire and how much of the plot it touched. This matters because in Spain burned forest land cannot change its use for 30 years. Small fires may not appear.

In Spain the report also checks livestock trails (vías pecuarias: cañadas, cordeles and veredas, old drovers' roads that are public property and cannot be fenced or occupied) and public forests and forests of public utility (montes públicos and montes de utilidad pública). In the United States, it checks public lands from the PAD-US database. They are a guide: the actual width of a livestock trail and the boundaries of a public forest are set by their official boundary survey (deslinde).

6. Land use and soil

The parcel's land-use units in SIGPAC (the official map of farm parcels used for subsidies), with the use recognised by the agricultural administration, their slope and their irrigation coefficient. Outside Spain, the report shows the land cover estimated from satellite imagery.

In any country, the report adds the soil type of the top 30 cm according to the global SoilGrids model: texture (sandy, loamy, clayey…), pH and organic matter. It is a guide at a resolution of 250 m, not a soil test.

7. Satellite history

The chart shows vegetation vigour (the NDVI index) month by month since 2017, from Sentinel-2 images. It runs from 0 to 1: close to 0 means bare soil, water or rock; above 0.6, dense, active vegetation. The normal pattern is an ebb and flow that repeats every year with the seasons.

What to look for are breaks in that pattern. The report marks sharp drops that last several months: they may be due to felling, a fire, ploughing up or building work, but also to mowing, drought or snow. It is a reason to ask questions, not proof. On very small or narrow plots, the reading picks up what lies around them.

8. Reference land price

What is paid on average per hectare for that type of land in the region, according to official statistics: in Spain, those of the Ministry of Agriculture by autonomous community and land class (rain-fed, irrigated, olive grove, vineyard, pasture…), applied to each SIGPAC land-use unit; in the rest of the European Union, Eurostat's figures by region; in the United States and Canada, those of their national statistics agencies. Where there are no public official statistics, the report says so. It is an average, not a valuation: the price of a particular plot depends on access, water, soil and the local market.

9. Questions

The list of questions for the town hall, the Land Registry, the river basin authority and the seller, depending on what has come up. It is the most useful part to take with you on a visit.

After the report

The buttons at the end let you compare the plot with what its listing says (area, price, irrigation, water, access and electricity), add it to a table to compare it with others, make an image to share it and print a checklist for the site visit. On your phone, “I'm on the land” makes the report for the spot where you are.

What is not included

The report does not include the detailed planning category or what the local plan allows to be built, nor registered charges, rights of way, water rights or a valuation of the plot. For those, see the checklist before buying.
