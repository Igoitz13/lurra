What to check before buying a rural plot

A listing for a rural plot tells you the area, the price and little else. This is the list of what is worth checking before you pay a deposit, ordered from the cheapest to check to the most expensive.

1. What you can see from home

With the cadastral reference, or by picking the parcel on the map, a Lurra report gives you in a minute:

  • Area according to the Catastro (the Spanish cadastre) and as measured on the outline, and whether they match.
  • Slope, mean and maximum, and what share of the plot is steeper than 20%: above that figure, working with machinery gets difficult.
  • Flooding: whether the plot is in a mapped flood zone.
  • Environmental protection: the Natura 2000 network and protected natural areas.
  • SIGPAC use: whether the agricultural administration, in SIGPAC (its official map of farm parcels), sees the land as arable, pasture, forest or unproductive, and whether it has recognised irrigation.
  • Planning classification, as a guide: whether the local plan records it as non-developable (rural), urban or developable land.
  • Satellite history: whether there has been felling, a fire or earthmoving since 2017.

That tells you whether it is worth going further.

2. The paperwork

  • Nota simple (an extract from the Registro de la Propiedad, the Land Registry): who the owner is, what area is recorded and what charges the plot carries (mortgages, seizures, easements, registered leases).
  • Catastro versus Land Registry: check that they describe the same plot. Differences in area are common and should be cleared up before the deed is signed.
  • Leases: if the plot is leased or lent to a farmer or livestock farmer, the contract remains in force after the sale and the tenant may have a pre-emptive right to buy.
  • Neighbours: on small rural plots, the owners of adjoining land may have a right of pre-emption (retracto). Ask the notary.

3. What you can do with it

  • Planning classification: ask the town hall for a planning report or certificate. It is the only document that tells you what can be built, and on rural land the usual answer is “almost nothing” or “only what is linked to the farm”.
  • Existing buildings: check that the shed, the farm building or the house has a licence or has been legalised. Appearing in the Catastro does not make them legal.
  • Subdivision: if you plan to split the plot, there is a minimum farming unit (unidad mínima de cultivo) below which this is not allowed.
  • Woodland: on forest land, felling and changes of use need authorisation, and burnt land cannot change from forest use for 30 years, with some exceptions.

4. Access and water

  • Access: confirm that you reach the plot by a public road or track or by a recorded right of way. A track across the neighbour's land that has been used “for as long as anyone can remember” is a problem waiting to happen.
  • Water: a well or water intake is only worth something if it is registered or has a concession from the river basin authority. On irrigated land, ask about the irrigation community (comunidad de regantes), its fees and its watering turns.
  • Electricity: the distance to the nearest line determines the cost of connecting.

5. On the ground

Visit the plot with the map in hand and walk the boundaries. Check that the boundary stones, fences or walls match the outline in the Catastro. Look at the soil after rain, and ask the neighbours: they usually know what is not written on any document.

6. When to call in a professional

If the area does not add up, there are buildings, you want to change the use or the investment is significant, an agricultural engineer, a forestry engineer or a land surveyor will pay for themselves. Take them the report and your list of questions: you will save time.
