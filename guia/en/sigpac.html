What SIGPAC is: what it is for and how to look up a parcel

SIGPAC (Sistema de Información Geográfica de Parcelas Agrícolas, Spain's geographic information system for agricultural parcels) is the official map of agricultural parcels in Spain: it shows where each parcel is, what it is used for, how steep it is and whether it is irrigated. The agricultural administration uses it to manage PAC payments (Política Agrícola Común, the EU's Common Agricultural Policy), and anyone can look it up online for free.

What it is for

Anyone applying for PAC payments declares their parcels on SIGPAC, and the administration uses it to check those payments. That is why it is very up to date: it is reviewed every year against recent aerial photos. For someone about to buy a plot, it is a quick way to see what the land is like: how much usable area it has, what is growing there, whether it has scrub or trees, whether it can be irrigated and how steep it is.

It covers the whole of Spain, including Navarre and the Basque Country. It is run by FEGA (Fondo Español de Garantía Agraria, the Spanish agricultural guarantee fund), part of the Ministry of Agriculture, together with the autonomous communities, which keep the data for their territory up to date.

Parcels and land-use units

SIGPAC starts from the parcels of the Catastro (the Spanish cadastre) and divides them into land-use units (recintos): continuous pieces of land with a single use. An olive-grove parcel with a threshing floor and a ravine may have three units. Each unit has its own area, use, mean slope and, where applicable, an irrigation coefficient.

They are named the same way as in the village: province, municipality, polygon, parcel and unit. That is why “polygon 7, parcel 45” works for both the Catastro and SIGPAC.

How to look it up

  1. Open the Ministry's SIGPAC viewer, which covers the whole of Spain, or your autonomous community's viewer (see the list below).
  2. Search for the parcel by province, municipality, polygon and parcel, or move around the map until you find it.
  3. Click on each unit to see its use, area, slope and irrigation coefficient.

With Lurra you do not need to: the “Land use according to SIGPAC” section of the report shows the parcel's units with that data, drawn on the aerial photo, alongside the Catastro, flood zones, protected areas and the satellite history.

The uses

Each unit carries a use code. The most common on rural plots are:

  • TA, arable land; TH, vegetable garden.
  • PS, grassland; PR, shrub pasture; PA, wooded pasture.
  • FO, forest.
  • OV, olive grove; VI, vineyard; FY, fruit trees; FS, nut trees; CI, citrus.
  • CA, roads and tracks; ED, buildings; AG, water; IM, unproductive; ZU, urban area.

The use reflects what the administration sees on the aerial photo, not what the local plan allows. A pasture plot (PS) does not become arable land just because you plough it: in protected areas or on forest land, ploughing up land needs authorisation.

What helps you when buying

  • Actual use: if the listing says “arable land” and SIGPAC says forest or shrub pasture, something does not add up.
  • Irrigation: the irrigation coefficient shows whether the administration recognises the unit as irrigable. Even so, the right to the water has to be checked with the irrigation community (comunidad de regantes) or the river basin authority.
  • Mean slope: a guide to how much of the land can be worked with machinery.
  • Pasture eligibility coefficient: on pasture with scrub or trees, what share counts as pasture for subsidies.
  • Usable area: the sum of the productive units, which can be considerably smaller than the area of the parcel.

What it is not

SIGPAC does not prove ownership, planning classification, water rights or PAC payment entitlements: those entitlements belong to whoever they have been allocated to and do not necessarily go with the land. If you are interested in receiving subsidies, ask the seller about the entitlements and how they are transferred.

Frequently asked questions

Are SIGPAC and the Catastro the same thing?

No. The Catastro is the tax inventory of properties: it says which parcel is which and what area it has. SIGPAC starts from those parcels and divides them into land-use units according to the use visible on the aerial photo, in order to manage farm subsidies. The guide to the Catastro for a rural plot explains what each of them gives you.

Does SIGPAC say who the owner is?

No. Neither SIGPAC nor the Catastro proves ownership: that is shown by the Registro de la Propiedad (the Land Registry), in the nota simple.

What do I do if SIGPAC contains an error?

Anyone with an interest, normally the owner or the farmer who declares the parcel, can ask their autonomous community to correct it, usually through the PAC payment application or through a specific SIGPAC amendment procedure.

Is there SIGPAC in Navarre and the Basque Country?

Yes. Although they have their own cadastres, SIGPAC covers the whole of Spain. Navarre and the Basque Country also have their own viewers.
