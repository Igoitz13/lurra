Can you build on a rural plot?

It is the first question almost everyone asks when looking at a rural plot, and the short answer is “very little, and it depends where”. This guide explains what it depends on and how to find out for certain before you pay.

Who decides

There is no single rule for the whole of Spain. What can be built on rural land is set by two sets of rules at once:

  • The land law of the region (comunidad autónoma): each region has its own, with its own permitted uses, minimum plot sizes and procedures.
  • The municipal plan (the plan general or the normas subsidiarias, the municipality's planning instruments): it classifies the land and, within rural land, distinguishes categories. “Ordinary” or “general” rural land allows some buildings; “protected” rural land (because of its agricultural, landscape or forestry value, or because of natural hazards) allows almost none.

What is usually allowed

  • Buildings linked to farming: storehouses, farm or livestock buildings, tool sheds. Normally with a maximum floor area and height, and sometimes only if the plot has a minimum size or there is a genuine farm.
  • Restoring existing buildings, such as a farmhouse or a mill, without extending them or with small extensions. This is usually more open than building from scratch.
  • Facilities of public or social interest (rural tourism, community facilities, energy), with prior authorisation from the region in addition to the municipal licence.
  • A new house: only in some regions and municipalities, and with conditions such as a large minimum plot, a link to a farm, or a minimum distance from other houses so that no new settlement takes shape. In many areas it is simply not allowed.

Prefabricated houses, modular units and mobile homes that stay permanently on the plot count as buildings: they need a licence just like a conventionally built house.

What comes on top

Even where the local plan allows it, other rules can prevent it or require a further authorisation:

  • Water: within 100 m of a watercourse (the control zone, or zona de policía) you need authorisation from the river basin authority, and building in flood zones is restricted. More in the guide to flood-prone plots.
  • Protected areas: in the Natura 2000 network or in a natural park, works must undergo an environmental assessment. More in the guide to Natura 2000.
  • Roads, coasts, public forests and drovers' roads: they have their own strips of land where you cannot build or where you need a permit.

Houses that are already there

A house appearing in the Catastro (the Spanish cadastre) or paying IBI (property tax) does not mean it has a licence. Before you buy:

  • Ask for the building licence and the first-occupation licence, or for a municipal certificate on the legal status of the building.
  • If it was built without a licence and the deadline for ordering its demolition has passed, many regions allow that situation to be recognised (for example, the “asimilado a fuera de ordenación” status in Andalucía). The house can be used and sold, but usually not extended. Each region sets the deadline, and on protected land there is often no time limit at all.
  • An extension, an enclosed porch or a swimming pool without a licence becomes the new owner's problem.

How to find out for certain

  1. With the cadastral reference, ask the town hall for a planning certificate or report (certificado or informe urbanístico) on the parcel: classification, category and permitted uses. Preferably in writing and before you sign anything.
  2. If the project is specific (a farm building of a certain size, a rural guesthouse), ask what authorisations it needs besides the licence and how long they take.
  3. If you can, write into the deposit contract (contrato de arras) that the purchase depends on getting that answer or that licence.

The Lurra report does not say what can be built, but it does give an indicative land classification from the Sistema de Información Urbana of the Ministerio de Vivienda (Spain's housing ministry), and it flags from the outset the constraints that weigh most: flood zones, protected areas, slope and recent earthmoving seen from satellite.
