A plot in the Natura 2000 network: what it means and how to check

More than a quarter of Spain lies within the Natura 2000 network, so it is common for a rural plot to fall inside it. That is not bad news in itself, but it changes the questions you need to ask.

What it is

Natura 2000 is the European network of sites for conserving habitats and species. It has two types of site, which often overlap:

  • ZEC or LIC: special areas of conservation and sites of community importance (SACs and SCIs), designated for their habitats and species.
  • ZEPA: special protection areas for birds (SPAs).

On top of these come the protected natural areas (espacios naturales protegidos) designated by each region (comunidad autónoma) or by the State: national and natural parks, reserves, natural monuments and protected landscapes. They are separate designations with their own rules, and they may coincide with Natura 2000 on the same ground.

What it means for a plot

Being in Natura 2000 does not in itself prohibit growing crops, keeping livestock or using the woodland as it has been used until now. What changes is that plans and projects that could significantly affect the site must undergo a specific environmental assessment. In practice this covers:

  • New buildings, farm sheds, tracks and fences.
  • Changes of use: ploughing up pasture, bringing in irrigation, turning woodland into cropland.
  • Felling, scrub clearance and earthmoving on any significant scale.

Each site has a management plan approved by the region, and that is where the specific answer lies: what is allowed outright, what needs a report and what will not be authorised. Parks and reserves also have their own management and use plans, which tend to be stricter and divide the area into zones.

One detail that affects the purchase: within a protected natural area, the administration may have a right of first refusal and pre-emption (tanteo y retracto) over the sale. Ask how it applies in your region before you sign.

It has advantages too

Some farming and forestry grants give priority to holdings in these areas or compensate them, and a protected landscape is an asset for rural tourism. It depends on each region and each call for applications.

How to check

The “Environmental protection” section of the Lurra report tells you which sites touch the parcel, with their name, official code, type of designation and the square metres affected, based on the Inventario Español del Patrimonio Natural y de la Biodiversidad (Spain's official inventory of natural heritage and biodiversity). With the code you can look up the management plan on the region's environment website.

In Spain the report also checks livestock trails (vías pecuarias, the old public drovers' roads) and public forests (montes públicos), which are public property or have their own legal regime. It does not include other protections that can also weigh on a plot, such as public water or coastal land, or the protected land categories in the municipal plan.

What to ask

  • The regional environment department: which management plan applies, and which zone does the parcel fall in? Does what I want to do need a report or an assessment?
  • The town hall: how does the local plan classify that land?
  • The seller: have you applied for permits before? With what result?
